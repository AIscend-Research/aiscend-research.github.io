ReScience C · Machine Learning

[Re] A Novel CNN Ablation Reveals Limited Fourier Inductive Bias in LiteFNO: A Reproducibility Study

We study LiteFNO, a lightweight Fourier Neural Operator for time-dependent PDEs, in two ways: as a reproducibility study, since no public implementation of the described architecture was available, and as a novel ablation that adds a parameter-matched low-rank CNN the original paper never includes, isolating the Fourier inductive bias. Our central question is whether the Fourier machinery earns its complexity. The responsible-AI stake is reproducibility itself: an architectural claim is worth only as much as the independent reimplementation that can check it.

ReScience C · Medical imaging

[¬Re] Association of genomic subtypes of lower-grade gliomas with shape features automatically extracted by a deep learning algorithm

We study the radiogenomic pipeline of Buda, Saha and Mazurowski (2019) in two ways: as a reproducibility study, since no public implementation was available, and as a stress test the original paper never runs, measuring how the reported association between tumor shape and genomic subtype survives when it is computed on automatically produced masks rather than manual ones. Our central question is whether the shape features are stable enough to carry the claim. In a clinical setting the answer decides whether a model’s output can responsibly be read as evidence about a patient.

ReScience C · Machine Learning

[Re] Invisible Farms: Reproducing and Stress-Testing Small-Scale Irrigation Detection for Zambia

We study the smallholder-irrigation mapping method of Boser et al. (2025) in two ways: as a reproduction, since the proposal reports no model metrics and its pipeline depends on gated Earth Engine access, so we rebuild it on an open no-credentials stack and report the first quantitative baselines, and as a stress test of the three choices it leaves open. Our central question is how much of the headline rests on defaults nobody set deliberately. Access is the point: climate-adaptation work is only usable by the people it describes if the pipeline runs without a credential and the reported gaps survive a proper test.

ReScience C · Computational Biology

[Re] Robust deep learning–based protein sequence design using ProteinMPNN

We study ProteinMPNN in two ways: as a reproduction of its core sequence-design results from the official code and weights, and as a robustness study the original paper never runs, perturbing backbones with coordinate noise, masking residues, and feeding deliberately malformed input. Our central question is what the model does when the structure it is handed is wrong. The failure modes are the responsible-AI finding: the more accurate model is not the more robust one, and most malformed inputs fail silently rather than raising an error.

ReScience C · Machine Learning

[Re] TB-Net: A Tailored, Self-Attention Deep Convolutional Neural Network Design for Detection of Tuberculosis Cases from Chest X-Ray Images

We study TB-Net (Wong et al., 2022), reported at 99.86% accuracy for tuberculosis detection, in two ways: as a replication, which published materials do not permit, since the architecture loads from a checkpoint whose download link is dead and the training cohort has changed since publication, and as a compression and robustness study aimed at smartphone deployment. Our central question is what survives the move from a headline number to a clinic. It is a health-access result: the models that would reach low-resource settings are the small ones, and we measure what shrinking them costs.

AI × Humanities · Alignment

Compulsory Hope: Instruction Tuning Teaches Poems to Evade, Not to Console

We study what alignment does to an ending in two ways: by cutting two hundred public-domain grief poems in half and having checkpoints that share one pretraining run and differ only in alignment stages finish them, and by sorting every ending under a blind rubric with the poets’ own endings shuffled in unlabelled. Our central question is whether a model tuned to be helpful learns to console or to look away. It is a concrete case of an alignment side effect that a sentiment score reads as an improvement.

Does the Decoding Algorithm Have a Voice? Measuring the Sampler’s Stylistic Signature Against the Prompt’s

We study the decoding algorithm, the greedy or temperature or nucleus setting a platform picks on the user’s behalf, in two ways: as a detection problem, asking whether a classifier can recover the sampler from style alone, and as a sizing problem, scoring that signature against the model’s and the prompt’s under one protocol. Our central question is how much of a generated text a silent default wrote. Interpretability here is a matter of credit: a knob the author never saw is a co-author nobody can currently account for.

AI × Humanities · Constraint

Erasure: A Stated Goal Reorganises What a Language Model Deletes

We study taste in two ways: by forbidding a model to add anything, so that its output must be a subsequence of the source and every word kept or cut is checkable, and by changing a single word of the instruction to see what the same operator does differently. Our central question is what a stated goal reorganises when nothing can be imported. Subtraction does not make a model more creative; it makes its judgments auditable, which is what interpretability of a creative system needs.

AI × Humanities · Recommenders

Exploration Slows Preference Capture Without Changing Which Preferences Are Reached

We study preference capture in two ways: as the drift measure the field already uses, scoring how far a recommender moves a user from an unmanipulated counterfactual, and as an endpoint measure we propose, asking not how far the user travelled but whose taste they arrived at. Our central question is what auditing by distance misses. The two readings disagree most where capture is most complete, so the standard audit returns its most reassuring numbers in exactly the wrong place.

AI × Humanities · Speech

I Am Speaking Into a Machine That Is Listening: Iterated Transcription as an Uncredited Co-Writer

We study automatic transcription in two ways: as a measurement, feeding a recognizer’s own output back in as the next performance after Lucier’s I Am Sitting in a Room, and as a test of the claim that generative loops collapse into a shared house style. Our central question is what a transcriber writes into a text that was never said. The stake is access: speech interfaces are how many people reach these systems at all, and the rewriting is small, permanent, and delivered at a confidence that never wavers.

AI × Humanities · Curation

Many Doors, One Room: Independent Venues Converge When Both Sides of a Creative Market Adapt

We study the obvious remedy for a narrowing gatekeeper, adding more gatekeepers, in two ways: as an agent-based market of independently initialized venues that each re-estimate taste from their own accepted work, and as a confound check, since holding published volume constant starves each venue into noise that only looks like independence. Our central question is whether plurality of venues delivers plurality of work. It bears on how cultural access is designed: what matters is not how many doors exist but whether each one learns from whom it already admitted.

AI × Humanities · Poetics

Restored Lines: A Model Finds the Poet’s Hardest Line Breaks and Declines to Write Them

We study the line break, the one poetic decision no rule fixes, in two ways: by stripping the lineation from 2,059 public-domain poems and asking a model to put it back, graded by how much syntax each break severs, and by asking the same model to write free verse of its own on matched subjects. Our central question is whether recognising a creative decision and making one are the same capacity. The gap between them says something practical about where these systems belong: closer to an instrument for reading than a collaborator in writing.

AI × Humanities · Co-writing

Style Gravity, in Text: How Long a Human-Authored Poem Opening Survives Inside a Model’s Continuation

We study the pull a model exerts on a human opening in two ways: as a measurement, scoring each continued line between the author’s style and the model’s house style on surface features alone, and as an audit of a withdrawn affordance, since the prefill channel this work depends on has been removed from the current Claude line. Our central question is what holds a poem where its author put it. The finding bears on platform power: an interface decision quietly changes how much of a co-written text stays the writer’s.

AI × Humanities · Curation

The Bottleneck: A Gatekeeper Trained on Its Own Accepted Work Narrows a Field While Making Its Bias Harder to Detect

We study the curator that forms its taste from work it has already accepted in two ways: as an agent-based simulation where the accepted set becomes the next round’s training corpus, and as a detection problem, measuring whether the resulting bias leaves a trace an auditor could find. Our central question is what a feedback loop with no channel for its own errors does to a field. The finding cuts against intuition and matters for fairness auditing: the filter that narrows most is the one whose exclusions are hardest to see.

AI × Humanities · Phonology

The Dead Ear: A Language Model’s Inherited Accent Is an Archive, Not an Author

We study inherited pronunciation in two ways: by building an item set from six dated sound changes, so that “these words once rhymed” follows from a documented event rather than a famous couplet, and by separating what a model reads off the page from what it hears. Our central question is whether accents nobody speaks still write. The answer bears on how we assign credit: a model can consult five centuries of verse accurately and still keep it out of its own writing.

AI × Humanities · Editing

The Unchosen Word: Language Models as Editors of Poetic Indeterminacy

We study models as editors in two ways: by putting four of them in the editor’s chair on 113 sites where Dickinson left alternative words standing and never chose between them, and by randomizing the manuscript’s own layout to see whether the decision is being made by the poem or by typography. Our central question is what a system’s demand for one fluent output does to meaning that was held open on purpose. It is a cultural-stewardship case: resolution that looks like editing is a redistribution of authority over the text.

AI × Humanities · Generative models

Unauthored by Design: The Seed’s Share of a Generated Artifact

We study the random seed in two ways: as a variance decomposition over a fully crossed prompt × model × seed design, and as a comparison run identically across image and text so the two modalities sit on the same footing. Our central question is how much of an artifact a person determined when they wrote the prompt. The answer matters wherever authorship carries weight, in credit, licensing, and disclosure: specification has a floor, and the seed is not quite noise.
